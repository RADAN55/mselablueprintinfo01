What the plan tells usLo que nos dice el plano

Review notesNotas de repaso

  • RL.3.1 / RI.3.1

    Refer explicitly to the text as the basis for every answer. The answer is on the page, not in your memory.

    Refiérete de manera explícita al texto como base de cada respuesta. La respuesta está en la página, no en tu memoria.

  • RL.3.2

    Recount fables, folktales, and myths. Find the central message, lesson, or moral and the key details that convey it.

    Vuelve a contar fábulas, cuentos populares y mitos. Encuentra el mensaje central, la lección o la moraleja y los detalles clave que lo transmiten.

  • RL.3.3

    Describe characters by their traits, motivations, and feelings, and explain how their actions contribute to the sequence of events.

    Describe a los personajes por sus rasgos, motivaciones y sentimientos, y explica cómo sus acciones contribuyen a la secuencia de sucesos.

  • RL.3.4 / L.3.5

    Tell literal language from nonliteral language. To take steps is not always to walk.

    Distingue el lenguaje literal del no literal. «Take steps» no siempre significa caminar.

  • RL.3.6 / RI.3.6

    Distinguish your own point of view from that of the narrator, the characters, or the author.

    Distingue tu propio punto de vista del que tienen el narrador, los personajes o el autor.

  • RI.3.2 / RI.3.3

    Determine the main idea and explain how key details support it. Describe events, ideas, or steps using words of time, sequence, and cause and effect.

    Determina la idea principal y explica cómo la apoyan los detalles clave. Describe sucesos, ideas o pasos con palabras de tiempo, secuencia y causa y efecto.

  • RI.3.8

    Explain the logical connection between sentences and paragraphs: comparison, cause and effect, or first, second, third.

    Explica la conexión lógica entre oraciones y párrafos: comparación, causa y efecto, o primero, segundo, tercero.

  • L.3.4

    A known affix unlocks a new word: agreeable, disagreeable. A shared root connects words: company, companion.

    Un afijo conocido abre una palabra nueva: agreeable, disagreeable. Una raíz compartida conecta palabras: company, companion.

Why the Mississippi River Floods

The Mississippi River is one of the longest rivers in North America. Rain and melting snow from many states drain into it. In spring, all that water arrives at about the same time.

First, the river rises inside its banks. Next, if the rain keeps falling, the water spills over the banks and onto the low land beside the river. This land is called a floodplain.

Floods can harm homes and roads. Because of this, people built tall walls of earth called levees to hold the water back. Floods are not all bad, though. When the water goes down, it leaves behind a layer of rich mud. As a result, the soil of the Mississippi Delta is some of the best farmland in the country.

Behind the Levee

Greenville, Mississippi, sits beside the great river. A tall levee protects the town. In 1927, after months of rain, a levee near Greenville broke, and water covered farms and streets for weeks. Afterward, people built the levees higher and stronger. Today families walk and ride bikes along the top.

The Last Word

My hands were ice when Mr. Okafor called my name. I walked to the microphone as if my shoes were full of sand. Only two of us were left: me and Danielle, who had won the spelling bee two years in a row.

“Your word is rhythm,” said Mr. Okafor.

My stomach dropped. I had missed that word on a quiz in September. After that quiz, I wrote it on a card and taped the card to the bathroom mirror. I saw it every morning for a month.

I closed my eyes and pictured the mirror. “R-H-Y-T-H-M,” I said.

“Correct.”

Danielle missed her next word, and I spelled mine. When Mr. Okafor announced the winner, I looked for my grandmother in the crowd. She was holding up a small white card, and she was grinning from ear to ear.

    Los textos informativos de este grado tienen dos o más ideas principales. Encuentra cada una y los detalles clave que la apoyan.

The Night Shift

When the sun goes down over Mississippi, a night shift begins. Thousands of bats leave barns, bridges, and hollow trees to hunt. Many people fear bats, but scientists say these animals are some of the most helpful neighbors a farmer can have.

A single bat can eat hundreds of insects in an hour. Many of those insects are moths whose caterpillars chew through corn and cotton. When bats eat the moths, farmers lose fewer crops and can spray fewer chemicals. Researchers have estimated that bats save American farmers billions of dollars every year.

Yet bats are in trouble. A disease called white-nose syndrome has killed millions of bats in North America. The disease wakes bats during their winter sleep, and they burn the fat they need to survive until spring. In addition, bats lose their homes when old trees are cut down and old buildings are sealed.

People can help. Some families put up wooden bat houses. Others simply leave dead trees standing when it is safe to do so.

Bat Count Night

June 14. Tonight I helped count bats at the old railroad bridge. We sat in lawn chairs with clickers and waited. At 8:12 the first bat dropped out of a crack and swooped over my head. Then they poured out like smoke. I clicked until my thumb hurt. Our total was 1,146. Last year it was 1,900.
