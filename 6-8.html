Review notesNotas de repaso

  • RL.6.1 / RI.6.1

    Cite textual evidence for what the text says directly and for the inferences you draw from it.

    Cita evidencia del texto tanto para lo que dice directamente como para las inferencias que sacas.

  • RL.6.2 / RI.6.2

    A summary stays distinct from personal opinions or judgments. If a sentence starts with 'I think', it is not a summary.

    Un resumen se mantiene aparte de opiniones o juicios personales. Si una oración empieza con «yo creo», no es un resumen.

  • RL.6.3

    Track how the plot unfolds in a series of episodes and how characters respond or change as the story moves toward a resolution.

    Sigue cómo se desarrolla la trama en episodios y cómo los personajes responden o cambian mientras la historia avanza hacia la resolución.

  • RL.6.5 / RI.6.5

    Explain how one sentence, paragraph, chapter, scene, or stanza fits into the overall structure and develops the ideas.

    Explica cómo una oración, párrafo, capítulo, escena o estrofa encaja en la estructura general y desarrolla las ideas.

  • RI.6.6

    Name the author's point of view or purpose and show how the text conveys it.

    Identifica el punto de vista o propósito del autor y muestra cómo el texto lo transmite.

  • RI.6.8

    Trace the argument. Separate claims that are supported by reasons and evidence from claims that are not.

    Sigue el argumento. Separa las afirmaciones apoyadas con razones y evidencia de las que no lo están.

  • L.6.5

    Connotation matters: stingy, scrimping, economical, and thrifty share a meaning but not a feeling. Personification appears here too.

    La connotación importa: palabras con un mismo significado pueden transmitir sentimientos distintos. Aquí también aparece la personificación.

The Catfish

Tomás had told everyone at school that he was an expert fisherman. So when his uncle handed him a cane pole at the edge of the pond, he took it as if he had held one a hundred times.

He had never held one.

For an hour the cork sat on the water like a button on a shirt. His cousin Lidia, who was nine, pulled in three bream. Tomás changed spots. He changed bait. He glared at the pond as if it had insulted him.

“You’re jerking the line,” Lidia said. “Let it sit. Wait till the cork goes all the way under.”

He wanted to tell her that experts did not take advice from fourth graders. Instead he heard himself say, “Show me.”

She showed him. Ten minutes later the cork vanished, and Tomás hauled a catfish onto the bank, slick and gray and furious. It was not a large fish. He held it up anyway, and when his uncle asked who had taught him, he pointed at Lidia before he could think of a better story.

ArgumentTexto argumentativo

Let Them Sleep

Every weekday, millions of American teenagers sit in first period before eight o’clock, and many of them are barely awake. Some adults blame phones and late-night games. That explanation is convenient, but it ignores biology.

During adolescence, the body’s internal clock shifts. The brain begins releasing melatonin, the hormone that signals sleep, later in the evening than it did in childhood. A teenager who lies down at nine-thirty may stare at the ceiling until eleven. When the alarm rings at six, that student has slept seven hours or fewer, well short of the eight to ten hours that pediatricians recommend.

The results show up in school. Districts that moved start times to eight-thirty or later have reported better attendance, fewer students falling asleep in class, and fewer car crashes involving teen drivers.

Critics answer that later start times scramble bus routes and push practices into the dark. These are real costs. They are also scheduling problems, and schedules can be rewritten. A teenager’s biology cannot. Besides, my own cousin’s grades went up the semester her school changed its schedule, which proves the policy works.

All week, Mrs. Beaumont’s third-period class had been planning. Jerome collected two dollars from each student. Aaliyah ordered the cake. Someone’s mother donated balloons, which were hidden in the band closet. After twenty-six years, Mrs. Beaumont was retiring, and she was not supposed to suspect a thing.

On Friday she stood at the board with her back to the room. “I have noticed,” she said, writing the date, “that this class has become very quiet lately. Quiet classes worry me.”

Nobody breathed.

“I have also noticed that Jerome, who has not turned in homework since October, has suddenly become interested in collecting things.” She capped the marker. “And Aaliyah asked me yesterday whether I prefer chocolate or lemon. For no reason at all, I am sure.”

She turned around. Her face was perfectly serious.

“Now, I am going to walk to the office to make some copies. It will take me exactly ten minutes. I expect that when I return I will be completely astonished.”

She picked up a single sheet of paper and left. The room exploded toward the band closet.

Irony depends on tone, and tone is the last thing a language learner hears. Read the teacher’s lines aloud twice, once sincerely and once dryly, and ask which reading fits the ending.

La ironía depende del tono, y el tono es lo último que capta quien aprende un idioma. Lee en voz alta las líneas de la maestra dos veces, una con sinceridad y otra con sequedad, y pregunta cuál encaja con el final.
