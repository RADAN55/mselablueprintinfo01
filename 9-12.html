Informational textTexto informativo

Up Highway 61

Ask where American popular music comes from, and sooner or later the road leads to the Mississippi Delta. The claim sounds like local pride. The evidence suggests it is closer to fact.

Begin with the conditions. In the early 1900s the Delta was cotton country, worked largely by Black sharecroppers who owned little and owed much. Field hollers and work songs, sung to set the pace of labor, were already in the air. So were church hymns. The blues drew on both, but it did something new: it put one singer and one guitar at the center and let that singer speak in the first person about trouble, love, and leaving.

Then follow the movement. During the Great Migration, hundreds of thousands of Black Mississippians went north, many of them up Highway 61 and the Illinois Central line to Chicago. The music went with them. In the city, musicians plugged in. Amplified Delta blues became Chicago blues, and within a generation young players in Britain and America were copying those records note for note and calling the result rock and roll.

The line is not perfectly straight; no line in music history is. But it is unbroken.

Three Minutes Before the Board

Members of the board, thank you for letting a sophomore take three minutes of your evening.

You have been told that closing the public library on Saturdays will save the county forty thousand dollars a year. I do not doubt the number. I doubt the arithmetic behind it, because it counts only what the county spends and nothing of what the county gets.

On Saturdays I have watched a man in work boots fill out job applications at the only free computers in town. I have watched a grandmother learn to video-call a grandson stationed overseas. I have watched children who have no books at home carry out as many as their arms can hold. None of that appears on a spreadsheet. All of it is what a library is for.

Some will say that everything is online now, so libraries are obsolete. Everything is online for those who can pay for the connection. In our county, one household in four cannot.

A supporter of the closure told me last week that anyone who opposes it simply does not understand budgets. I understand that budgets are choices. A budget says, in numbers, what a community believes is worth keeping.

Keep the doors open. Five days are not enough when the sixth is the only day some of our neighbors are free.

In Praise of the Group Project

Let us take a moment to celebrate the group project, that noble invention by which four students are assigned a task and one of them completes it.

The benefits are plain. The group project teaches cooperation, in the sense that three people cooperate in waiting. It teaches communication, chiefly through messages sent at 11:40 p.m. that begin, “Hey, so what are we doing?” It teaches leadership, for someone must eventually sigh, open the document, and lead.

Consider also its fairness. Each member receives the same grade, a practice that would be admired in any workplace if only workplaces were wise enough to pay everyone the salary of the person who did the work.

Critics complain that the system rewards idleness. This is shortsighted. The idle member is learning a valuable lesson, namely, that idleness is rewarded, and he will carry it with him into a long and restful career.

I therefore propose that we expand the practice. Let final exams be taken in groups of four, with one pencil.

The Other One

The house sold in March, which meant that by the time I drove down in June there was nothing of ours left to see but the pecan tree, and a stranger’s truck in the drive.

I parked across the road anyway.

My grandfather planted that tree the year my mother was born. He used to say it was the only thing on the property that had never once asked him for money. Every fall he cracked pecans on the porch steps with a hammer, badly, sending shell across the yard, and every fall my grandmother told him there was a tool for that, and he told her he was holding it.

A woman came out of the house and looked at my car. I raised a hand. She raised one back, the way people do here, asking nothing.

I had meant to knock. I had a speech ready about who I was and whose tree that was. But she went to the mailbox and back inside, and the screen door clapped shut behind her with the exact sound it had always made, and I found that I had already heard what I came for.

I left the engine off a while longer. Then I went home, to the other one.
